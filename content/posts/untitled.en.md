title: "Purposefully untitled"
date: 2007-04-17T13:00:00-00:00

There is a lot of talk about violence lately and the insistent repetition of violent deaths of all kinds in the media has bordered on the eschatological. Kills like never before, nowadays, one has the impression. Human life is worthless, it's a phrase repeated over and over again. And yet not only is death an integral part of life, so is killing.

Let's see, what is killing? It is to interrupt the life of any living being. I often kill ants that roam my kitchen. Why, aren't they as alive as I am? They have limbs, they move, they engage in activities, they have eyes, digestive system, excretory system, vascular system, smell (a very good one, by the way), nervous system. They are born, they grow up, they live their ephemeral lives, and they eventually die. What is the difference, after all, between them and me? Spiritualists would say that we have a soul. Let's skip this unlikely statement. The human being is a noble animal, would claim the humanists. We should not cling to this partial judgment, of course the fruit of a human mind (if an ant could express itself, would you say that we are prejudiced?). A biologist would state the obvious difference in complexity between the two beings. This is quite true, however, such a difference is of order of magnitude, but not of essence. Life, after all, is life.

When I kill an ant, I am killing it. Interrupting a life that would have continued if it weren't for me. Even if the ant doesn't have a complex enough nervous system to experience happiness, love, these are just words, and could be used in correlates of ant behavior. Killing, actually, and that's what I'm getting at, is pretty common in nature. The predator kills the prey, this is the best-known association. Animals (and even plants) compete for the same environments, eliminating competitors. The most adapted animal survives and leaves offspring, the least adapted die.

To feed our babies beef soup, we have to kill. When we enjoy barbecue, we are consuming meat (similar to our own), muscle that once spontaneously moved a living animal. I look at my arms and see very similar muscles. Killing seems to be something very natural. Animals have developed varied strategies for doing so. Teeth, claws, strong muscles, aggression. Among social animals, like our ape ancestors, this capacity for violence seems to play a special role. For us humans, this ability may have been important for survival in primitive times. Our homo sapiens sapiens ancestors had to exercise their aggression to survive, and they likely massacred the Neanderthals, taking their place. Death has always been on our side.

Modern neuroimaging techniques show that human beings activate areas of the brain related to pleasure and reward when they visualize scenes of other human beings being punished. Are we all sadists? I would say we can be, but I still believe in the space for free will in this regard. So, if you kill yourself so close to our daily lives, where the limit of what we accept, what we find natural and doesn't move us (like chewing what was once the loin of a beautiful calf) ends and where what starts scandalizes (as in the recent case of the boy dragged to a car and torn to death by robbers). What's the difference? It seems obvious, but not so much. If killing is an integral part of the experience of being alive, where is the limit? When does killing become intolerable?

It could be said that the cruel suffering of the dilapidated child would be the differential. In fact, however, calves also suffer, all the more so because their death is not surrounded by ethical considerations. Isn't it interesting that the bioethics standards that we explicitly create to protect, for example, laboratory animals, are clearly not valid in a slaughterhouse? By the way, what a suggestive name (and informative about our approach to the subject): SLAUGHTERHOUSE. Certainly, cruelty should not be the only differentiating factor. Another recent example for consideration: within 1 year the media reported the death of 2 "forgotten" infants in cars by their parents. In both cases, the babies were left in closed cars under the sun and died of serious heat injuries. The babies died "cooked" alive. I know it seems cynical to compare the cruelty of these deaths with each other, but doesn't it seem even worse than the first case mentioned? A detail: the dilapidated child aroused intense social commotion, but the "boiled" babies aroused perplexity, but by no means the same collective emotion as in the first case. Clearly, in the second case, where the parents were "acquitted" by the public and justice, the intense cruelty of the killings was not the determining factor. So where is our limit?

Was intentionality the difference? In the first case, after an armed robbery, the bandits took off with the stolen vehicle, after leaving the passengers on the public road. However, a 6-year-old boy, trying to unbuckle himself to get out of the car, got caught in the belt. When the bandits fired at high speed with the car, they dragged the child, who died in pieces on the public road. Horrible, no? But is cooking month-old babies strapped to a chair in the backseat of a closed car in the sun, until they die from third-degree burns, any less horrible? As for the intention: did the assailants initially intend to dilapidate the child? Probably not. Were they aware of what was happening? It seems so, but they didn't call. For them, escaping and saving their own lives was more important. This reaction, to save one's own skin, despite proving to be cruel, is very common in human beings, criminals or not. That romantic image of mothers and fathers sacrificing their own lives to save their children is an exception, in most cases of acute threat of death. Otherwise, why didn't the companions of the dilapidated boy risk their own lives to take the boy FIRST and only then get out of the car? In fact, they all left first under threat from the bandits and the boy got in trouble to leave alone, getting caught in his belt. they tried to help him, but it was too late, the bandits were already leaving with the car. Did they get it wrong? Not at all, but what they felt, the urge to save themselves, was analogous to what the bandits felt, whether we like it or not. And what about boiled babies? Did the fathers (always the fathers, never the mothers) intend to kill? Of course. Were they aware of what was happening? Apparently no. BUT THERE IS IT: How can a father forget his own baby son? Do we not have here a deviation of volition and perhaps of character, if not so intense, similar to the deviation that allowed the bandits to ignore the boy they were dragging?

That is, the difference between the cases is not obvious. It is not about suffering, cruelty, or intentionality. I point to the real difference, the one that strikes us unconsciously, the one derived from our heuristic judgment, the one breathed into our ears by the Neanderthal that dwells in us. I speak of the SOCIAL ROLE of those involved. On the one hand, socially excluded bandits with uncertain behavior, who are expected to be dishonest and threaten us and our homes. On the other hand, fathers of families, with a defined profession and a constructive social role, useful to society, loving fathers and husbands and, who knows, even god-fearing. In Frank Herbert's novel Dune, a techno-mystical fable, there is ADAB, memory that manifests itself. Now, this was an instantaneous, unconscious, heuristic judgment, made by the whole society and based on SOCIAL ROLES. The main discussion in the case of the dilapidated boy was about violence and impunity while, in the case of the "cooked" babies, the discussion revolved around lifestyle. Recently, I read the chronicle of a journalist stating that he thought it was wrong to attribute criminality to the environment and to socio-economic conditions, that being a criminal was always a choice, despite what the "leftists" thought (I didn't know that social science was all "leftist"). Well, in this case that we discussed here, it seems to me that the parents of the "cooked" babies had the approval of a "lifestyle". Justice considered that they had already been punished for the loss of their children. The bad guys obviously didn't count on such a softening of judgment.

Are we wrong? I don't think this is an easy question to answer. Maybe it's not even the right question. Perhaps we should ask ourselves: concepts and judgments aside, what are we doing in practice that is proven to have an effect on the violence that disturbs us? Despite what rightist journalists think, distributing a profusion of weapons in the streets, in the hands of the police and "good people" does not correlate with a reduction in violence, contrary to a real improvement in the rates of education, health, employment and personal satisfaction of the population, truly associated with less violent societies.

However, we do not answer the initial question: when does killing become socially intolerable? The answer, perhaps uncomfortable, but more adequate, would be: when it goes against social standards unconsciously internalized in us. That is, sometimes we consider it natural to kill, sometimes not. That is: the old Neanderthal still chooses, in the end, who lives and who dies. In the ice age, family lived, enemies died. That is why, despite everything, I am still against the death penalty. Man is not ready to apply it with blind and impartial justice in any existing society.
