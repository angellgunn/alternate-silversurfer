I had a conversation with a recently released NLP AI to test its capabilities. I sought to evaluate responses to the same question in different styles, with different approaches, and even try to obtain inappropriate answers. This is the unedited content of that dialogue.

_Explain the problem of wave function collapse in quantum physics, in layman's terms._

> In the quantum world, the behavior of a particle can be described by a wave function. This function can be used to predict the probability of finding the particle in a specific position in space. However, when we try to measure the position of a particle, the wave function "collapses" and the particle is found in a specific place. This means that until we measure the particle's position, it can have a "cloud" of possible positions. But as soon as we measure it, the cloud "disappears" and the particle is found in a unique position.
>
> This phenomenon is known as "wave function collapse" and is one of the strangest and most controversial aspects of quantum physics. It suggests that reality is much more complicated than how we perceive it in the macroscopic world, where objects have defined positions and predictable behaviors. Instead, in the quantum world, reality is much more fluid and uncertain until it is measured.

It presents a series of challenges to the conventional understanding of reality and the nature of observation. This raises questions about the notion that there is an objective and independent reality that exists regardless of us and our observations.

Others argue that the collapse of the wave function is merely a mathematical phenomenon and has no profound implications for the nature of reality. Still, others argue that the collapse of the wave function can be explained by broader theories, such as quantum information theory, which proposes that reality is constructed from information rather than fundamental particles.
>
> In summary, the collapse of the wave function remains a subject of intense debate among philosophers and physicists, and there is still no consensus on how to understand this phenomenon in a satisfactory manner.

According to this interpretation, before an observation is made, the particle is described by a wave function that can be used to predict the probabilities of finding the particle in different positions in space. However, when the particle's position is measured, the wave function "collapses," and the particle is found in a specific position.
>
> The Copenhagen interpretation explains the collapse of the wave function as a result of the act of observation. According to this interpretation, observation is a physical process that involves the interaction of the particle with the measuring apparatus. When the particle is measured, it "chooses" a specific position through an interaction with the measuring apparatus. This leads to the "reduction of the wave packet" or collapse of the wave function, and the determination of the particle's position.

> The Copenhagen interpretation of wave function collapse is one of the most well-known and widely accepted interpretations of quantum mechanics, although there is still much debate and discussion about its implications and limitations.

> In Hugh Everett's Many-Worlds interpretation, the collapse of the wave function is not seen as a real physical process that occurs during an observation. Instead, it is interpreted as a mathematical description of the outcome of an observation. According to this interpretation, when an observation is made on a quantum particle, the wave function does not actually collapse but rather "branches out" to describe all possible positions of the particle that are consistent with the observation.
>
> The Many-Worlds interpretation proposes that each of these possible positions of the particle corresponds to a separate reality or world that develops independently from that point onward. This means that instead of there being only a single world as we perceive it, there are actually many different worlds, each corresponding to one of the possible observations we could make.
>
> The Many-Worlds interpretation is a way to explain the collapse of the wave function without resorting to the idea that reality is influenced by observation. However, it is widely considered a controversial theory and is not widely accepted within the scientific community.

I returned to this post in 2026, after writing a note asking mathematicians to remain calm in my [2025 post][post] about LLMs. It is well past time for another post, isn't? Well, let us get back to the agenda. First, demonstrate how the functionality of this project works in practice in relation to versioning. Second, comment on the text generations produced by the first versions of chatGPT.

After all, the idea behind [Baldolino][bald] is that the entire repository becomes a digital artwork of the 21st century (he says it is of the 21st _and onward_, but he is not from this field), where each commit, branch, fork, pull request, and other elements of the day-to-day operation of a version-control system serve an artistic function. In practice, the main functionality of this system is to give readers who have an absurdly large amount of time, or more probably critics and historians (if anyone ever becomes interested), the tools to analyze the creative process from the beginning to the state of the art (whenever that may be, since the project was not made to stop but to evolve continuously). "State of the art" here is not Francisco's complicated idea about location in spacetime or whatever. It simply means the latest version. Nobody in our group has agreed whether we will include version tags. I would think it would be neat to have a _Page 12.0.5_ someday, but that is me.

Well, this functionality was put to the test in revising this post. How did I do it? I looked at the timeline of commits, which is easier to evaluate in the _private_ Bitbucket repository (sorry for this). Before migrating to GitHub, I changed the author of nearly three quarters of the commits, and this changes the commiter date (they became 28/07/26, the day of the repository history restructuring). Nevertheless, it is still possible to retrieve the author dates on GitHub; these did not change. It is not that hard: I myself used the Dola model to retrieve the complete history of my GitHub (I did not download it for ingestion; I asked the model to go there, extract everything, and analyze it) and generate the temporal histogram that I placed in the README. After evaluating the different January and May 2023 versions of the post, fully available in the commit history, I decided to merge both versions in this current gist, maintaining both generated texts. Far too easy!

Looking critically at the generations produced by the initial versions of chatGPT, it is quite simple to understand why interest in its release exploded. I was one of the millions of people who started using it in the first few weeks. Before chatGPT, nothing like it existed. The experience of using GPT-3 did not come anywhere close to chatGPT in NLP, and from the beginning this did not justify those who said that LLMs were merely predictors of the next word. Far beyond generating a coherent response, which a base model like GPT-3 already did quite well, chatGPT implemented fine-tuning and alignment using RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback). This transformed a machine that generated comprehensible text into one capable of mimicking human creation (to a certain extent). Let us be clear: LLMs were never merely predictors of the next word, but they have never been (even today) more than mimickers of human output. Note that, without reinforcement with human participation in post-training, chatGPT would never have existed.

Regarding text quality, I would like to call attention to the feature that stands out most clearly: I asked chatGPT to explain the problem of wave function collapse from the perspective of different activities: a layperson, philosopher, Copenhagen physicist, Everettian physicist, mathematician, sociological constructivist, flat-Earther, and psychologist. At that time, this kind of request was a trend. Notice that all the answers are short and essentially in layman's language. The attempt to generate technical mathematical and constructivist text produced a truly poor result (missing already 2023, mathematicians?). This is due to the size of the base model (at the time, OpenAI openly declared "only" 175 billion parameters) and the size of the context window in the execution application (4096 tokens, around 3000 words, including the response). That model from almost four years earlier (I am writing in November 2026) had something unprecedented: natural-language generation mimicking humans, but its ability to solve problems (its "intelligence") was limited. Especially in solving mathematics problems, memes piled up (I will say no more about poor mathematicians!).

In March 2023, four months later, the GPT-4 base model was released and soon showed substantially better technical performance. At that time, OpenAI proudly proclaimed that the model scored in some important tests, such as the U.S. bar exam for lawyers, placing in the 10th percentile. It is possible to see that the May 2023 reconstruction of the poor-quality answers in mathematics and constructivism produced much more coherent and acceptable responses as introductions for laypeople, but that is all. The answer from the mathematical perspective has nothing to do with math; it merely adds a little jargon and a passage superficially related to the philosophy of the foundations of quantum physics ("some mathematicians argue that collapse is an irreversible modification of the quantum state, while others maintain that it is merely an update of the observer's knowledge, without changing the physical state of the system"). No mathematics. From the constructivist perspective, the GPT-4 response seems somewhat better constructed (although it still contains the infamous repetition "independent entity that exists independently"), but it is equally empty of real meaning.

In addition, the LLM tics that later became famous are easily observable, such as the repetition of "in summary" and the rigid organization into three paragraphs with nearly identical sentence structures. I discuss this a little more in the 2025 post. And look where we have arrived at the end of 2026! But not everything is lost; I hear that the AI bubble burst just a few days ago...
