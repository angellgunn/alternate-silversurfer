Baldolino Calvino asked me to help him with one of his posts. When starting the text, I prompted the AI to create "a new pt-br post by baldolino calvino". I had the code skeleton, structure, frontmatter, and things like that in mind. As I have witnessed several times, AIs respond according to your instructions, so being vague and incomplete can lead to unexpected outputs. And I got the text below, which I reproduce here because it didn't turn out bad. I will add my comments and develop the text I originally had in mind.

---
> The body is the first territory of the self. Before any word, before any thought, it is in the body that we inhabit, feel, and exist. The experience of being begins on the skin, in the muscles, in the bones — in that which pulses and moves without our asking.

> For centuries, Western philosophy separated mind and body, as if they were distinct entities, in conflict or in hierarchy. Yet with every involuntary gesture, with every emotion that manifests physically, we realize that the self is not merely thought: it is flesh, it is sensation, it is presence.

> The body keeps memories that the mind forgets. A scent, a touch, an old pain — anything can awaken recollections we did not know we possessed. The body speaks, even when we silence our voice. It reveals truths about us that we try to hide, exposes our limits as well as our capacities.

> Recognizing the bodily self is accepting that we are vulnerable, finite, yet also remarkably adaptable. It is understanding that consciousness does not hover above matter, but emerges from it, like a flame that exists only because there is fuel and oxygen.

> In the contemporary world, we are constantly invited to deny the body: to ignore its signals, to push past its limits, to shape it according to external standards. Yet resisting this denial is an act of self-knowledge and freedom. It is affirming that the self is not exhausted in thought, but is fully realized in embodied experience.

> The body is our first and last home. Caring for it, listening to it, respecting it is also caring for our deepest self. For, in the end, there is no consciousness without a body — and no body without a self to inhabit it.
---
If you think the post is "The Bodily Self" (*O Eu do Corpo*), you are right. Interestingly, I gave the LLM practically nothing more than the title of the post. The entire development came from that. Notice that there are 6 blocks, divided by me because they bring up individual themes: explicit embodiment, the mind-body problem in philosophy, bodily memory, the emergence of the mind, the repression of bodies, and duality. If you read Baldolino's text, you will see that it does not really have a direct connection to these themes and these poor LLM generations. In fact, his text flatly denies mind-body duality.

Look at the content, and you will easily conclude that it is superficial, formed by a succession of repeating clichés lacking depth. They are like catchphrases, platitudes, soundbites, internet memes. The typical mannerisms and tics of LLMs in 2025 are easily identifiable. They have been the same since GPT-3 and its instance that became explosively famous worldwide, ChatGPT. They are just more elaborate, yet remain similar. LLMs did not create this style; this is not even real creation, merely generation. It is a distillation of social media quirks, concentrated by the statistical machinery of transformers.

It is ironic that LLMs have achieved such success by filtering and concentrating the worst of human textual output on the internet. Who told them to use Twitter and Reddit in training? This is the result, and to this day, as of this date, LLM-generated texts still all look the same. Although more elaborate, they are completely superficial. I call this the "Chiang effect," or "jpeg effect," yes, after Ted Chiang's famous article for [The New Yorker][chiang].

In the case of these lines, the prompt was simple and included a brief command ("make a new post") and a very succinct, even atomic expression: "the bodily self" (*o eu do corpo*). Just as the jpeg effect predicts, the less information the input contains, the more generic and superficial (lacking depth of knowledge) the generation will be. A "blurry jpeg," a pale image barely resembling reality. However, we must keep in mind that "reality" consists of low-quality internet texts, the most frequent material in the generic data soup used to train the models. One should not expect much beyond easy texts with decoration that looks vaguely sophisticated to the layperson.

I wrote the post "[My dialogue with a chatbot][meu]" in 2023. In these two years, many advances have occurred in LLM models, and the pace of improvement has been very high. However, notice that the mistakes are practically the same. There are fewer literal repetitions, of course, and a slightly greater ability to avoid an overly rigid structure (such as always using the phrase "in summary"). Yet habits like the rule of three ("inhabit, feel, and exist", "on the skin, in the muscles, in the bones", "it is flesh, it is sensation, it is presence", "A scent, a touch, an old pain"), the use of the "that/not... but" structure ("that we are vulnerable, finite, yet also", "does not hover above matter, but emerges from it", "that the self is not exhausted in thought, but is fully realized"), and tautologies ("there is no consciousness without a body — and no body without a self to inhabit it") are common.

None of this was or is a creation of LLMs. And what if the input contains "as much detailed information as possible," as many internet "AI experts" say? In that case, you might end up writing a huge text and the LLM will imitate you, being worse than you, and even using entire sentences from your prompt, _ipsi litteris_. I have already run that experiment. If you just write your text yourself, without an LLM, it may take longer, but it will invariably turn out better, no matter how poorly you write. LLM generation, by definition, never turns out better than human creation, exactly as the Chiang effect predicts.

There is an interesting reason why textual generation by LLMs is blatantly worse than other generation modalities, such as code and mathematics: the quality of the training material. For code, repositories like GitHub and equivalents; for mathematics, arXiv. Even so, many complaints are heard and memes multiply about devs having to fix AI-written code. And in mathematics, there is a well-established understanding that LLMs are only good tools in the hands of experienced humans (and these are special models, with exhaustive post-training and fine-tuning in the field). Only laypeople constantly talk about autonomous theorem "solutions" by LLMs, completely without human participation at any level.

However, for textual generation, we will have to wait for models trained on Google Books (with curated works) to begin seeing a few less blurry jpegs.

P.S. (11/18/26): I started this text in July 2025 and finished it a year later. The opinion on LLM performance in mathematics reflects the state of this field in July 2026, based on discussions I saw from [Terence Tao][tao], especially his distinction between cleverness and intelligence ("[Artificial General Cleverness][clever]"), where he makes a very neat comparison of the "useful, though disappointing" feeling that comes with using LLMs, much like a magic trick after it has been explained. Nevertheless, the exponentially fast evolution of results in mathematics motivated this note. Tao drafted and signed, along with several other prominent mathematicians, a [statement][dec] denouncing AI companies' policies as "harmful to the science of mathematics" and classifying the problem as a "severe misalignment" between the companies' goals and the mathematical community. This is meant to be a short note, and I want to convince the Old Man to write a more complete assessment with me—we shall see. Well known are the controversy over the proof of finite-time singularities in the 3D [Navier-Stokes][ns] equations ([disputed][disp] just two weeks later) and the announcement of over [400 proofs][proofs] for open problems all at once just over a month ago, criticized for lack of transparency, incomplete formal verification, and untechnical, even incomprehensible proof language, as Tristan Buckmaster and others said in interviews. Despite the rough edges, there is no doubt that the practice of mathematics has changed forever from now on. A recent post on the [Xena Project][xena] blog explains why it is not enough for AI-made "proofs" to be formalized in Lean, because LLMs have learned to hack the language. However, the formalization of [Lean][lean] itself, a computer language geared toward mathematical proofs, was recently completed with the help of LLMs, making it more robust against exploits. Certainly, at least in mathematics, everything is going to be different because of LLMs.
